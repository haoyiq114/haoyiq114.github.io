TL;DR

Recurrent transformers pass a latent state from one token to the next. They are a new kind of architecture, and we wanted to know what happens when you post-train one with existing RL methods: GRPO, with and without the published corrections for rollout–trainer mismatch, synchronously and asynchronously. Our model follows the Latent Recurrent Transformer (LRT) design. It reads the prompt and trains with a few parallel passes over the sequence, but generates with a per-token recurrence, so it carries a built-in training–inference mismatch, and how large it is depends on how many passes the rollout and the trainer run. At the default pass counts, plain GRPO trains through an epoch. With fewer passes, or in asynchronous training when rollouts come from weights refreshed only every 16 updates, it collapses, and the per-token log-probability gap between trainer and rollout in the first 20 updates already tells the two cases apart. Score centering keeps training stable in every pass-count setting at no cost in accuracy, and the methods built on it stay stable with stale rollout weights as well.

A model that disagrees with itself

Recurrence is back in transformers. Looped transformers such as Inkling 2 Mini and GLM-5.4 run some layers several times per token. Recurrent transformers (LRT, T2MLR, the full-bandwidth transformer, LIFT and other latent feedback transformers) recur across tokens instead: each token passes a latent state to the next. Most of the attention so far has gone to architecture and pretraining; RL post-training has barely been touched. This post takes one recurrent transformer, a model built on the Latent Recurrent Transformer (LRT) design, and asks a plain question: what happens when you post-train it with the RL methods people already use?

We did not design a new algorithm. We ran GRPO as it is, alone and with the published corrections for a mismatch between rollout and trainer (truncated importance sampling, score centering, and combinations built on them), both with fresh rollout weights and with stale ones, as in asynchronous training.

One property of our recurrent transformer makes this more than a routine check. It trains with a few parallel refinement passes over the whole sequence, but generates text recurrently, one token at a time. RL post-training assumes that the policy that generates the rollouts and the policy the trainer updates are identical. In a standard transformer they differ only slightly, through numerical differences between inference and training code or through stale weights in asynchronous pipelines. In our recurrent transformer the difference is built in, and its size depends on two numbers we choose: how many passes the rollout spends reading the prompt, and how many passes the trainer uses to score the response. The LRT paper notes this mismatch itself (§4.6) and reduces it by starting the trainer from the rollout's cached states, and two earlier notes by one of us work through what it means for the policy gradient (policy consistency, drift). We summarize that argument below and then measure the mismatch. Across our settings, the per-token log-probability gap between rollout and trainer runs from 0.004 to 0.073.

Here is what we saw:

  • Plain GRPO trains only when both sides run enough passes. With at least 2 prefill passes and at least 3 training passes, which includes the default of 3 and 3, uncorrected GRPO trains through an epoch. In the other 10 of 16 settings it collapses. Matching the two pass counts is not enough: prefill 2 with a 2-pass trainer collapses too.
  • The early gap separates the two groups. Every uncorrected run that trained through the epoch, synchronous or asynchronous, started with a per-token gap of 0.011 or less over its first 20 updates; every one that collapsed started at 0.019 or more.
  • Score centering keeps the recurrent transformer stable under its built-in mismatch, at no cost in accuracy. Every score-centering (SC) variant kept all 16 settings stable, at the same 63–67 on GSM8K that uncorrected GRPO reaches when it survives. TIS alone slips with a one-pass trainer.
  • With stale rollout weights on top, the methods built on SC still hold. Refreshing rollout weights every 2 or 4 updates is harmless. Every 16 updates collapses uncorrected GRPO and every 64 also breaks TIS, while SC, SC+TIS and SC+KL+mask stay stable at 64, although by then they no longer gain on GSM8K.
  • One epoch of RL raised consistency, not the ceiling. With 8 samples per problem, average accuracy rose 10–13 points, but the share of problems solved at least once rose only about 2.

How a recurrent transformer trains and how it generates

In a standard transformer, the first layer of a new token never sees what the last layer concluded about the previous one. A recurrent transformer adds that pathway. Ours follows the LRT design, which adds it to a standard decoder-only transformer: the hidden state of token t at a chosen source layer is fed into the layers of token t+1, through the attention keys and values and the residual stream (Huang et al., 2026). At inference this memory is free, because the state was already computed for the previous token. Each new token still costs one forward pass. That is the appeal: in the paper, the model matches the language-modeling loss of a transformer that runs its layers three times per token, while decoding in one pass per token with about 9% extra latency.

Training cannot follow that chain token by token: unrolling it exactly would be backpropagation through time over every sequence, and would throw away the parallelism that makes pretraining fast. The paper trains with a parallel approximation instead, and our model uses the variant it calls full-sequence refinement. An initialization pass processes the whole sequence with no memory. Each refinement pass then recomputes every position in parallel, reading the memory that the previous pass produced one position to its left. In the paper, the loss is averaged over all passes.

The consequence is the whole story of this post. With K training passes, memory can travel at most K − 1 positions, so training sees a truncated version of the recurrence. Decoding runs the recurrence in full: it behaves like a parallel forward with as many passes as there are tokens. The model you train and the model you sample from are cousins, not twins. The prompt sits in between. It is read with P parallel prefill passes, then decoding continues from the last prompt state.

Our model uses the paper's 20-layer configuration (width 1,280, 10 attention heads, 2,048-token context, 32,768-token vocabulary) with a mixture-of-experts MLP: 8 experts with 2 active per token, plus one shared expert. It follows the paper's pretraining recipe (the nanochat stack on FineWeb-Edu) for 18,887 steps with 3 passes, initialization plus 2 refinements. Fine-tuning keeps the same 3 passes and runs on OpenMathInstruct-2 (the 5M split) for 4,734 steps of 524,288 tokens. RL starts from that checkpoint.

How this differs from a looped transformer. A looped transformer such as Inkling 2 Mini recurs in depth: it runs its middle layers three times on the same token before emitting it. That costs more compute per token at inference, but training unrolls exactly the same loops, so rollouts and training compute the same function, up to the usual numerical noise. A recurrent transformer recurs across tokens instead. Ours pays for its extra passes only in training, and trains on an approximation of what it runs at inference. That approximation is the knob.

What the mismatch should do to RL

Before the measurements, here is what existing theory says to expect. Nothing here is new: the decomposition is from the score-centering paper, and its application to a recurrent transformer follows an earlier note by one of us.

RL assumes one policy; in practice there can be two. Call q the policy that sampled the rollouts and p the policy the trainer differentiates. Write for the reward of a rollout , 1 if its final answer is correct and 0 otherwise. RL wants the gradient of the expected reward under q. When the two differ, the trainer computes something else:

A check with equal rewards shows what goes wrong. Suppose every rollout earns the same positive reward. Nothing separates a better response from a worse one, so the weights should not move, and when q = p they do not, on average. When the two differ they do. With equal rewards the covariance vanishes and only the drift is left. It says nothing about which token was better, and because the updated weights produce the next rollouts, it accumulates from one update to the next. GRPO's baseline removes it in the equal-reward case but not in general: subtracting a problem's mean reward makes the advantages sum to zero over that problem's rollouts, not at each prefix.

Score centering removes the drift and nothing else. Score centering (SC; Marek and Ryabinin, 2026) replaces with , which deletes the drift term and keeps the covariance. It does not change how p or q is computed, so the two stay as far apart as before.

In a recurrent transformer the mismatch is part of the model. A standard transformer computes q and p the same way in a different order. Ours does not: q reads the prompt with P prefill passes and then runs the exact recurrence, and p approximates it with K parallel passes. Replay is the fix on that side: it hands the trainer the rollout's recurrent states, so p matches q in the forward pass.

That gives three things to look for:

  1. A gap before any update. At identical weights, p and q should already give different probabilities to the same token, by an amount that shrinks as K grows (Measuring the mismatch).
  2. Uncorrected GRPO breaks where the gap is large. The theory does not say how large is too large; the runs put the line between 0.011 and 0.019 (Sync RL).
  3. SC stabilizes training without closing the gap, and the same should hold when stale weights add to the mismatch (Sync RL, Async RL).

Measuring the mismatch

On every training batch we log the gap, the mean absolute difference between the trainer's and the rollout's log-probability of each response token. A gap of 0.01 means the two are on average about 1% apart in the probability they give a token. We average it over the first 20 updates, long before any run collapses. Across the 16 settings it runs from 0.004 to 0.073. Runs with a correction start within about 12% of these values, so the early gap is a property of the setting, not of the loss: the figure below shows the same gaps under score centering (SC), the correction introduced above, whose loss is given in the next section.

  • The trainer's pass count matters most. With one training pass, the memory pathway is never used, and the gap is about 0.07 whatever the rollout did. With the prefill matched to the trainer, 2, 3 and 4 passes bring it to 0.019, 0.007 and 0.004.
  • For a given trainer, the gap is smallest when the prefill uses the same number of passes. This holds in all four columns. More prefill passes than training passes do not help: with a 2-pass trainer the gap rises from 0.019 at prefill 2 to 0.023 at prefill 4.
  • A one-pass prefill keeps the gap at 0.036 or more, however many passes the trainer runs. The rollout read the prompt without memory, and no number of training passes reproduces that.
  • Replay almost closes the gap. Feeding the trainer the recurrent states the rollout actually produced, the fix proposed in the LRT paper, gives 0.003 in every row. We attribute what remains to numerical differences between the two code paths.
  • Score centering leaves the gap where it is. With SC, the 16 early gaps are within 8% of the uncorrected ones (0.007 in the standard setting either way), and over the last 20 updates of the epoch they are still at 1.0–1.9 times their starting values. Yet none of the 16 SC runs collapsed, so SC does not work by shrinking the gap. It removes the drift term that the mismatch adds to the update and leaves the two paths as far apart as before, as the decomposition above predicts.

The six settings at 0.010 or below, two or more prefill passes with three or more training passes, are the ones where uncorrected GRPO trains. The standard setting, prefill 3 with a 3-pass trainer, sits at 0.007.

Sync RL: which settings collapse, and what fixes them

Main takeaway

In synchronous GRPO, the pass counts determined whether uncorrected training survived the epoch. Uncorrected runs collapse in 10 of 16 settings. Every correction we tried keeps all 16 alive, but only the ones built on SC also keep their accuracy.

Each run is one epoch of GRPO from the same fine-tuned checkpoint, with every rollout sampled from the latest weights, and each setting was run once. The data are 5,192 GSM8K and MATH (levels 1–3) training problems that the starting model solves 20–80% of the time. One epoch uses 4,864 of them, as 304 updates of 16 problems × 16 rollouts, at temperature 1 with at most 768 new tokens, and a rollout earns reward 1 when its final answer is correct. Between runs, only the rollout's prefill passes, the trainer's passes and the loss change.

We compared four corrections to the per-token GRPO loss. For each response token y, p is the trainer's next-token distribution, q the rollout's, and A the advantage: the response's reward minus the mean reward of its problem's 16 rollouts.

  • SC (score centering) subtracts the drift term: it makes each token's score average to zero under the rollout's own distribution.
  • TIS (truncated importance sampling) reweights each token toward the trainer's distribution and caps the weight, trading some bias for bounded variance.
  • SC+TIS applies both: the capped importance weight on the sampled token, and the centering term with the same weights.
  • SC+KL also pulls p toward q, our simplified reading of Tinker's StableReinforce loss.
  • Replay is not a loss. It changes how p is computed: the trainer reads the recurrent states the rollout produced, so p matches q from the start. We ran it with the uncorrected loss.

In this run, training is healthy for about 90 updates with the gap near 0.02. Then the gap grows by nearly three orders of magnitude, answers stop finishing within the length limit, and rollout accuracy is near zero by update 157. The SC run uses the same setting, data and order, and its gap stays near 0.02 throughout.

Training accuracy tells the same story as the gap. In the standard setting the five curves overlap: they climb from the SFT checkpoint's 59% to about 78% within 100 updates and end at 79–81%. Under a one-pass prefill, again with a 3-pass trainer, the corrected runs end at 73–78%, while the uncorrected run peaks at 74% around update 80 and falls to 5%. Among the uncorrected runs, a larger starting gap also meant an earlier collapse: training accuracy fell to half its peak at updates 121–149 with a 2-pass trainer and at least 2 prefill passes (gap 0.019–0.023), at 111–117 with a one-pass prefill and at least 2 training passes (0.036–0.040), and at 73–83 with a 1-pass trainer (0.068–0.073).

We evaluate every final model greedily on the GSM8K test set (1,319 problems) and MATH-500, reading the answer from the last boxed expression and grading MATH-500 with math-verify. All synchronous runs are evaluated with prefill 3, whatever prefill they trained with, so the numbers compare directly. One score carries sampling noise of about 1.3 points on GSM8K and 2.2 on MATH-500 (one standard error), so differences of a point or two between cells mean little.

The collapsed settings are exactly the ones whose gap sat at 0.019 or above in the heatmap: every one- or two-pass trainer, and a one-pass prefill with any trainer. With a correction, every setting trains. GSM8K lands at 63–67, the same range as uncorrected GRPO where it survives and up to 4 points above the starting 62.9. MATH-500 stays at about 39–45 (start 41.8).

The one weak spot is TIS alone with a one-pass trainer, which ends at 55–62 on GSM8K, 3–9 points below SC, and at 27–38 on MATH-500, below the start on both. In synchronous training a correction is insurance, not an improvement: it buys stability, and the accuracy you get is the accuracy you would have had without the mismatch.

Scored with the prefill each model trained with instead (switch above), the picture is the same, except that corrected runs with a one-pass prefill score a few points lower, because the evaluation itself reads the prompt in one pass (their SFT start is 58.0 rather than 62.9).

The two blocks on the right of the figure are reference runs, both with 3 training passes: replay, where the trainer reads the recurrent states the rollout produced (no correction), and an exact rollout that reads the prompt token by token instead of in prefill passes (no correction and SC). All of them train normally and land at 64–67 on GSM8K, in the same range as the corrected runs.

Async RL: how much staleness each method survives

Main takeaway

In asynchronous GRPO, rollouts come from weights a few updates old; this staleness adds to the gap and does what missing passes do. In the standard setting, uncorrected runs survive a lag of 4 updates and collapse at 16; TIS survives 16 but breaks at 64; every method built on SC survives 64.

We emulate asynchronous training by sampling rollouts from a frozen copy of the weights that is refreshed every N updates (N = 2, 4, 16, 64), so the rollout policy is up to N − 1 updates old, with no queue and no rollout cut off midway. For scale, the Beyond Score Centering post describes a common open-source setup with a batch age of about 31, and the setting of the score-centering paper with a staleness of 64. Everything else matches the synchronous runs, in the standard setting and in the one-pass-prefill stress setting.

Staleness shows up in the gap we measured above. Refreshing every 2, 4 and 16 updates raises the standard setting's gap from 0.007 to 0.008, 0.011 and 0.035; the last is about what a one-pass prefill gives (0.037).

Besides the four corrections above, we ran a fifth built for asynchronous training, from Beyond Score Centering. It takes the place of plain SC+KL, which we did not rerun with stale weights. It adds to SC a KL penalty whose weight shrinks with the age a of the rollout policy, and drops every position where the two distributions already differ by more than τ:

The training curves show how stale weights break a run. With refreshes every 64 updates, rollouts come from the starting model for the first 64 updates, so training accuracy stays near 59% while the trainer drifts. At the first refresh the uncorrected run falls to zero and TIS to about 15%. SC+TIS jumps to 77%, while SC and SC+KL+mask sit at about 50–65% for most of the run and reach 72–77% near the end. TIS fails on its own but helps on top of SC: SC+TIS is the only method that recovers right at the first refresh, in the stress setting as well. With refreshes every 16 updates, only the uncorrected run degrades, sliding from 68% to 18%.

The scores after one epoch tell the same story. In the standard setting, lags of 2 and 4 cost nothing: every method ends at 64–66 on GSM8K, as in synchronous training. A lag of 16 collapses uncorrected GRPO. A lag of 64 also collapses TIS, while SC, SC+TIS and SC+KL+mask end at 61–63, a few points below their synchronous results and no higher than the SFT checkpoint's 62.9. At this lag RL stays stable but gains nothing on GSM8K, although training accuracy still ends at 72–77%. Under a one-pass prefill, uncorrected GRPO collapses at every lag, and the corrected methods behave as in the standard setting.

What RL did not do here

Once training was stable, one epoch of RL made the model more consistent but barely more capable. With 8 samples per problem at temperature 1, it raised the average accuracy of a sample by 10–13 points but the share of problems solved at least once by only about 2. We measured this on two models from the standard setting, the SC run shown here and the SC+TIS run, and they give the same picture.

In these runs, RL made answers the model could already reach by sampling come up more often, but added few new ones. This is also why the greedy scores in the heatmaps move so little: before RL a sample at temperature 1 was far worse than the greedy answer (49.9 against 62.9 on GSM8K), and after RL the two are nearly level (63.2 against 64.4). Raising the ceiling is the subject of the next post.

Future work

  • Looped transformers. Inkling 2 Mini runs its middle layers three times per token, and Thinking Machines released a variable-loop version for research that keeps improving on serial reasoning out to ten loops, twice as many as it was trained with. That makes it tempting to roll out with more loops than the trainer uses. A looped model run that way should show the same kind of mismatch, and the same per-token gap may tell whether RL survives. This is a guess we have not tested.
  • Real asynchronous systems. Our emulation refreshes frozen weights on a fixed schedule. Real pipelines keep a buffer of rollouts of different ages and have to choose which to train on first; the Beyond Score Centering post finds that this choice matters.
  • Numerical mismatch in standard transformers. Different implementations for rollout and training open the same kind of gap (Yao et al., 2025), and so do quantized rollouts, which the score-centering paper tests. Whether the early gap separates the runs that train from the ones that collapse there too is a cheap check.
  • Replay with gradients through the memory. Our replay feeds the rollout's recurrent states to the trainer without gradients. Letting gradients flow through them would let RL train the recurrence itself.
  • Seeds and longer runs. Every setting here is one run of one epoch. Repeats would show how sharp the split between settings is, and longer runs whether the settings that trained keep training.
  • Raising the ceiling. In our runs RL mostly raised the average sample, while pass@8 barely moved. The next post asks which SFT and RL data raise pass@k itself.
